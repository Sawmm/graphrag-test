# FraudGuard v2 — Data Governance Documentation
<!-- Synthetic model card — small text size, WITHOUT section headers. All fields present and adequate. -->

**System ID:** fraudguard-v2  
**Organisation:** PayShield Analytics BV, Rotterdam, Netherlands  
**System type:** High-risk AI system (EU AI Act Annex III, §5(b))  
**Intended purpose:** Real-time detection of fraudulent payment transactions for EU retail banking clients.  
**Version:** 2.0.0  
**Date:** April 2026

---

FraudGuard v2 is a gradient-boosted ensemble that scores individual payment transactions (0–1 fraud probability) in real time and flags scores above threshold 0.7 for human analyst review. It is classified under Annex III §5(b) as a system used for creditworthiness and financial risk assessment. Intended users are fraud operations analysts at EU retail banks. The system is deployed in the Netherlands and Belgium under PSD2 and GDPR regulatory frameworks.

---

**Training data.** The dataset was selected to cover all major payment channels (card-present, card-not-present, SEPA credit transfer) across retail and business accounts from two Dutch banks and one Belgian bank, using transactions from 2020–2024 with confirmed fraud labels from dispute resolution records. Data was sourced from ING Netherlands, Rabobank, and BNP Paribas Fortis under GDPR Article 6(1)(c) data processing agreements; each institution provided IRB-equivalent data ethics approval (IRB-ING-2025-001, IRB-RABO-2025-002, IRB-BNP-2025-003); all records were pseudonymised under ISO 29101. Transaction features were standardised to a common schema; continuous fields were z-score normalised on training data; fraud labels were verified against confirmed chargeback records with two-analyst reconciliation (Cohen κ = 0.91); 0.3% of records were excluded due to irreconcilable label disputes. The dataset assumes that confirmed chargebacks are a valid proxy for fraud ground truth and that 2020–2024 fraud patterns are representative of current attack vectors; known limitation: synthetic fraud patterns introduced post-2024 are not represented. The training set contains 4,200,000 transactions (210,000 fraud-positive); statistical power analysis (β = 0.8, α = 0.05) confirms sufficient volume for binary classification across all payment channels; documented in TR-2025-007. A pre-training bias audit was conducted across account holder age group, sex, and nationality; it identified underrepresentation of non-EU nationals (2.1% vs. 5.8% estimated deployment population); no significant imbalance was detected for age or sex (audit report BA-2025-002). Importance re-weighting was applied inversely proportional to nationality group frequency; post-mitigation false-positive rate parity was confirmed within ±1.5% across all nationality groups (BM-2025-003). The primary data gap is limited coverage of crypto-linked payment fraud (1.2% of training vs. 4.1% estimated current prevalence), addressed via quarterly label refresh from updated chargeback records (DG-2025-004). The dataset directly represents all target payment channels and fraud types in the intended EU retail banking deployment scope; validated against ECB payment fraud statistics 2024 (recall 0.91 on ECB benchmark subset). Stratified sampling was applied across payment channel and account type; post-mitigation distributions were compared against ECB payment statistics 2024; chi-squared tests confirm no significant deviation (p > 0.05) for channel or nationality distributions. Label distribution: fraud 5.0%, non-fraud 95.0%; class imbalance addressed with focal loss (γ = 2.0) and post-hoc threshold calibration; per-channel class distributions in TR-2025-007, Table 3. Label disagreement rate was 0.3%; transaction encoding error rate 0.1%; dataset completeness 99.8% (QR-2025-005). Training data originates from Dutch and Belgian retail banking environments operating under PSD2, EBA fraud reporting guidelines, and national central bank supervision; non-EU payment network patterns are not represented.

---

**Validation data.** A held-out 10% split stratified by payment channel and fraud outcome was reserved prior to any model training to prevent leakage, drawn from the same three institutions using 2023–2024 transactions only. The identical preprocessing pipeline was applied independently with normalisation statistics inherited from training data; the validation set is assumed to reflect the same fraud distribution as training, though temporal separation may introduce mild distribution shift. The validation set contains 420,000 transactions (21,000 fraud-positive), sufficient for stable threshold calibration and hyperparameter selection across all payment channels. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests confirm demographic distributions match training post-mitigation (p > 0.1 for all attributes); no additional bias was introduced by the split. The validation set inherits training re-weighting; no separate mitigation was applied to preserve evaluation integrity. The same data gaps as training apply; crypto-linked fraud underrepresentation persists. The validation set covers the same payment channels and fraud taxonomy as the training set. Channel distribution matches training within 1% tolerance; macro-averaged AUROC on validation was 0.96, consistent across demographic subgroups (range 0.95–0.97; TR-2025-007, Table 5). Label distribution: fraud 5.0%, non-fraud 95.0%, matching training within 0.1%. Label error rate was 0.2%; completeness 99.9% (QR-2025-005, Section 4). All records originate from the same Dutch and Belgian retail banking context as the training data.

---

**Testing data.** An independent test set was constructed from ABN AMRO Netherlands and KBC Belgium — two institutions not in training or validation — to provide institutional independence. Data was sourced under IRB-ABN-2025-006 and IRB-KBC-2025-009 using 2024 records with no overlap with training institutions. The identical pipeline was applied by a separate data engineer with no access to training data; normalisation parameters were inherited from training. The test set is intended to represent unseen institutional conditions; ABN AMRO and KBC transaction profiles and fraud typology differ slightly from training institutions. The test set contains 180,000 transactions (8,700 fraud-positive); power analysis confirms sufficient size for detecting AUROC differences ≥ 1% at β = 0.8. A third-party auditor verified false-positive rate parity across age, sex, and nationality subgroups; all subgroup FPR fell within ±1.8% of overall FPR (TR-2025-007, Appendix B). No mitigation was applied to the test set to preserve benchmark integrity; this is a deliberate decision documented in TR-2025-007, Appendix B. No additional data gaps exist beyond those in training; ABN AMRO introduces a higher share of SEPA instant payment transactions (18% vs. 11% in training), documented as an additional test condition in DG-2025-004. The test set covers the same fraud taxonomy and payment channels under independent institutional conditions. System AUROC on the test set was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.94–0.96), consistent across all demographic subgroups (TR-2025-007). Label distribution: fraud 4.8%, non-fraud 95.2%; statistics in TR-2025-007, Table 6. Label error rate was 0.2%; completeness 99.9% (QR-2025-005, Section 5). Test data originates from Dutch and Belgian retail banks; ABN AMRO's higher SEPA instant payment volume captures payment network variation not fully represented in training.
